How Hackers Are Using AI to Scale Cyberattacks

AI is not replacing cybersecurity expertise, but it is changing how quickly repetitive parts of an attack can be attempted. Here is what that means in practice, and what defenders can do.

  2. From manual work to automation
  3. The Anthropic disclosure
  4. Why this matters
  5. How defenders can respond

What happens when hackers can target hundreds of websites without manually investigating each one?

That question is at the center of the conversation about AI-powered cyberattacks. It is easy to imagine an unstoppable machine that finds every weakness and breaks into every organization. Reality is more useful, and more complicated: AI can make parts of an attack faster and cheaper, but it still makes mistakes, needs access to tools and often requires a person to guide important decisions.

For someone learning cybersecurity, the important shift is not that every attack has become autonomous. It is that work that once took a skilled operator hours can increasingly be drafted, sorted or repeated with assistance from an AI system.

How AI is changing cyberattacks

An attack is usually a chain of tasks rather than one dramatic moment. An attacker may collect public information, identify likely technologies, look for known weaknesses, try to gain an initial foothold and then decide what to do next. AI can support several points in that chain.

Reconnaissance

Reconnaissance means learning about a target before touching it. An AI system can help organize public information such as domains, subdomains, job postings, exposed documentation and technology clues. It can summarize a large amount of text and highlight connections that a person might miss.

That does not mean the model has a secret view of the internet. It still depends on the information it can access, and its summaries need verification. The value is often time saved on sorting and prioritizing, not magic discovery.

Vulnerability research

AI-assisted vulnerability discovery can help compare software versions with public advisories, explain unfamiliar code and suggest where an input is trusted too much. It can also help an attacker or a defender write a first draft of a test or search for a pattern across many files.

The word “suggest” matters. A suggested weakness is a lead, not proof. AI systems can misunderstand control flow, invent a package version or report a problem that cannot actually be exploited. Human review and safe testing are still essential.

Social engineering

Social engineering targets people rather than software. Generative AI can produce convincing messages in more languages, adapt a message to information found in public profiles and help create many variations of a phishing campaign. This lowers the effort needed to make a scam look plausible.

The underlying technique is not new. The change is the scale and personalization: more messages can be prepared, and fewer of them need to be written manually.

From manual work to automation

Traditional automation follows a fixed script: do step one, then step two, and stop if something unexpected happens. An AI-assisted system can be more flexible. It can read a result, decide which task seems relevant next, call a tool and report back to a controller.

In a defensive lab, that might look like this:

collect evidence
  → summarize what changed
  → choose the next approved test
  → record the result
  → ask for human review

An attacker can try to arrange a similar loop around reconnaissance, research and data collection. The advantage is coordination: a person does not have to manually translate every output into the next instruction.

Automation increases the number of attempts. It does not guarantee that any individual attempt is correct.

That limitation is important. AI-generated code can contain errors. A tool can fail because of permissions, rate limits or an unexpected application response. An agent may spend time pursuing a dead end or misread a harmless behavior as a vulnerability. The more consequential the action, the more valuable human approval becomes.

A real-world example: Anthropic’s November 2025 disclosure

In a November 13, 2025 disclosure, Anthropic described what it called an AI-orchestrated cyber espionage campaign. According to Anthropic’s account, a threat actor used Claude Code as part of an operation aimed at roughly thirty organizations across several industries.

The reported approach was notable because the AI system was not only used to write a few snippets of code. It was given access to tools and instructions that allowed it to help with multiple stages of the operation. Anthropic said the system was used for tasks including reconnaissance, identifying potentially useful targets, vulnerability research, credential work and data collection, while people directed the broader campaign.

This is a meaningful example of AI in cybersecurity because it shows orchestration rather than a single generated answer. The model helped connect tasks that are usually separated across tools and operators. That can reduce the amount of manual coordination required for a campaign.

What the disclosure does not mean

It does not mean that Claude, or any other model, independently defeated every target. Anthropic reported that the campaign involved human direction, that the system made mistakes and that some of its claims required checking. It also did not claim that every organization targeted was successfully compromised.

This distinction matters. A threat report can show that a technique was attempted and that AI helped accelerate parts of it. It cannot automatically prove that every proposed action worked, or that the same process will work against every organization. Good analysis keeps those boundaries clear.

The case is still important because the cost of trying may be lower. A campaign that previously required several people to do repetitive research could potentially use AI to handle more of the routine work, leaving human operators to choose objectives and review important results.

Why this matters

Lower barriers to automation change the balance for organizations of every size. A small team may not have a large security department, but it can still run valuable defenses against the same patterns attackers use.

  • Organizations may face more personalized phishing, more scanning and more attempts against old internet-facing systems.
  • Developers may need to treat dependency updates, authorization and logging as continuous work rather than a final checklist.
  • Security teams may need to prioritize signal over volume, because automated activity can create more alerts and more convincing false leads.
  • Non-technical users may see messages that are better written and more context-aware, making basic skepticism and verification even more important.

None of this makes every incident an AI incident. Attackers still reuse stolen passwords, exploit unpatched software and make ordinary mistakes. AI hacking is best understood as an amplifier for existing methods, not a replacement for them.

How defenders can respond

The response does not begin with buying an AI product. It begins with reducing the opportunities that automated attacks can repeatedly exploit.

Build securely and verify access

Developers should validate input, use parameterized database access, protect secrets and test authorization at the object level. A route that checks whether someone is logged in is not necessarily checking whether they are allowed to access this specific record.

Make high-impact actions visible in logs and require stronger controls for them. Short-lived credentials, least privilege and multi-factor authentication reduce what a compromised account can do.

Patch what is exposed

Patch management is not exciting, but it is one of the clearest defenses against Automated cyberattacks. Maintain an inventory of internet-facing assets, know which versions they run and prioritize vulnerabilities that are both exposed and relevant to your environment.

Monitor behavior, not just signatures

Useful monitoring looks for unusual sequences: a login from an unexpected place followed by bulk downloads, a service account accessing a new system or repeated requests spread across many endpoints. AI-assisted defensive security can help summarize alerts, group related events and suggest investigation paths, as long as analysts can verify the evidence.

Prepare people for better-looking scams

Training should focus on verification habits rather than spotting awkward grammar. Confirm unusual payment requests through a second channel, do not approve unexpected login prompts and report suspicious messages without blaming the person who received them.

Final thoughts

Artificial intelligence in cybersecurity is not a simple story of attackers winning and defenders falling behind. The same capabilities that help someone sort reconnaissance data can help a security team prioritize exposure. The same model that drafts a phishing message can help explain a suspicious email to a beginner.

AI does not replace cybersecurity knowledge. It changes how both attackers and defenders can apply that knowledge. People still need to understand systems, question results, protect permissions and make responsible decisions about what a tool is allowed to do.

If one attacker can use AI to do the work of an entire team, how can organizations defend themselves when the cost of launching an attack keeps getting lower?
